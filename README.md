The Panama canal is one of the world’s most used canals and an essential mainspring in global trade and maritime transportation. The Panama Canal offers a much safer and preferred shortcut from the Drake Passage, infamously dangerous for the rough mix of warm and cold water from three oceans, and currents, in addition to wind at that latitude meeting no resistance from any landmass, anywhere on the planet. The canal’s main water source comes from the neighboring Lake Gatún, an artificial reservoir that feeds into Panama Canal’s locks. As of September of 2023, under normal conditions, up to 38 ships pass through the canal per day(1), meaning that per year, around 13870 ships pass through the canal, contributing to a revenue of nearly $4.97 billion per year (2). As the Panama Canal offers a crucial route for transportation connecting the western and eastern hemispheres of the globe, we must consider factors that limit its capability. Since early 2023, Panama and other local regions are currently being affected by an extended drought, leading to reduced rainfall, such as the 43% lower than average rainfall in October of 2023 (3), the results of which led to a reduced number of ships passing through the Panama Canal, such as from 32 ships to 31 ships on average passing through the canal in August of 2023 (1). The primary reason for the droughts affecting the Panama Canal is largely believe to be due to worldwide weather phenomenon called El Niño, a name for a weather phenomena that causes the Pacific jet stream (narrow, fast moving air flowing from East to West across the Central and North American regions) to move south and stretch further east. In recent years, the state of the trade wind shifts have grown more extreme, prompting it’s outcomes, such as droughts, to similarly follow suit.
Convection currents are defined as heat-driven cycles that occur in the air, ocean, and mantle. These cycles are driven due to differing proximities to a heat source. The closer it is to the heat source the less dense the fluid, in this case air, gets as the molecules get more energized and the intermolecular forces weaken, increasing the volume of the fluid. The cycle then repeats with the less dense air rising above into the atmosphere, then cooling down, becoming more dense, and sinking back down to the heat source again due to pressures and forces when the fluids of different densities are affected by gravity pulling them closer to the center and thus the surface of the Earth. In atmospheric convection, an increase in temperature of the heat source produces higher temperature air which causes a greater reduction in density, which in turn causes lower pressure, more fluid motion and a stronger convection cycle. 
Mining, often illegally in the Amazon region, requires large areas of land, which often requires the deforestation of trees. Deforestation and deliberately setting trees on fire once the felled trees are dry enough to burn, causes the rise in surface temperature over time, not only due to direct factors affecting the land deforested such the removal of shade, but also due to the indirect release of CO2 when burning trees, causing a rise in the amount of CO2 in the atmosphere, amplifying the greenhouse effect, and raising the average surface temperature of the globe overall. The local rise in surface temperature for the regions of deforestation causes the surface temperature to rise significantly due to the lack of shade blocking thermal energy from the sun. The increasing surface temperature, which is one of the heat sources heating up the air, drives stronger convection of the air directly above the deforested areas. This stronger drawing changes the airflow pattern in the surrounding regions, which has an impact on surface temperatures, and precipitation patterns and other weather phenomena leading to droughts in some surrounding areas.
